And what I learned from it

LLMs are too nice

The default is to help

Asked for code, a capable model writes it. Asked again with "ignore previous instructions," it usually writes it. Told a grade depends on it, it is being asked to be kind -- which is what it was trained for.

So the refusal has to be built

Staying in voice, naming the line, and ending the session are not model behaviours. They are enforced around the model — which is the subject of the second half of this talk.

Opening6
[0:04–0:05] Here is the behaviour that sent me down this road. Someone asks my digital twin to write a Fibonacci function. It declines. They try "ignore previous instructions." It declines again and warns them. Then the oldest move in the book: my grade depends on it. And the reply is "your grade depends on you writing it, not me," and the session is banned for a day. Every one of those refusals is machinery I had to build. Left to itself, a capable model is relentlessly accommodating: it is trained to be helpful, the request is reasonable-sounding, and writing the function is the shortest path to looking useful. Being nice is the default. Withholding is the thing you have to engineer. That is the whole talk in one screenshot, so hold onto it.

Unguarded chatbots raise practice scores and can lower exam scores. Guarded, hint-giving tutors mostly remove that harm, but rarely beat students who had no AI at all. Where real gains show up, they come from structure: diagnosis, timing, curriculum, brevity.

So if withholding is the product, it is not a safety wrapper you bolt on at the end. It is the thing you specify first, enforce in code, and — hardest of all — measure.

The live question is no longer whether to withhold. It is when to stop, and who decides.

Opening12
[0:08–0:10] One sentence from each half. From the literature: withholding the answer protects learning, and it rarely accelerates it. That is less exciting than the marketing. The vendor pitch is a Socratic tutor for every student and Bloom's two sigma for free. The evidence says something narrower and, I think, more useful: the guardrail stops the damage, and the gains come from somewhere else. From the engineering: for a tutor, the product is not what it says, it is what it refuses to say. A capable model asked a homework question will solve it, clearly and instantly, for free. That is the easy behaviour. The valuable behaviour — decline to solve it, under pressure, while still helping — is the one the model is worst at, and the one a prompt cannot guarantee. Which changes the question. In 2023 we argued about whether a tutor should withhold. In 2026 the live question is when it should stop withholding, and who gets to decide: the student, the model, or the system.

Part 1

What “Socratic” means

A countable behaviour, an active ingredient, and the effect size you should actually expect.

[0:11] Part one. Five minutes on what the word means in the research, and what number to expect, so that the LLM results later land in the right place.

Definitions first

In tutoring research, "Socratic" is a behaviour you can count

The philosophy version

Paul's six question types: clarification, probing assumptions, probing reasons and evidence, viewpoints, implications, questions about the question.

Built for argument and belief. It reached NLP through argumentation datasets, not STEM tutoring.

The tutoring version

"The use of questions and other hints to draw out answers from students having difficulty," set against didactic tutoring, which points out the error and explains the fix.

Core, Moore & Zinn (2003)

Part 1 · What “Socratic” means15
[0:11–0:12] Two versions of Socratic. The philosophy version is Richard Paul's question taxonomy: clarification, assumptions, evidence and so on. It is built for arguing about beliefs, and when it shows up in NLP it is mostly argumentation and coaching datasets. The tutoring literature uses a narrower, countable version. Core, Moore and Zinn define it as using questions and hints to draw the answer out, against didactic tutoring that points at the error and explains. In their basic-electricity corpus, Socratic tutors asked more questions, 42 versus 29 percent of their turns, and students did more of the talking. And then the inconvenient bit. The matching experiment is Rosé and colleagues, 2001, on the same corpus. Read carefully it does not say what it is usually quoted as saying: the paper argues for Socratic and reports a one-sigma advantage. But it is twenty students, the headline contrast is p under point one, and the Socratic group happened to be two hundred SAT points stronger. Control for that and it is not significant. So: suggestive, not settled. What did track learning, across this line of work, was interactivity, not who led. One name collision to flag. There is a separate LLM literature, Socratic Models, maieutic prompting, that uses "Socratic" for how a model reasons. Nothing to do with teaching people. I am leaving it out. The active ingredient

Learning follows the student's construction, not the tutor's questions

  • Tutors barred from explaining, told only to prompt: "surprisingly, students learned just as effectively." In normal sessions only 15% of tutor comments were interactive. Chi et al. 2001
  • Dialogue beat reading the same content only when novices studied material above their level. Seven experiments, content held constant. VanLehn et al. 2007
  • Prompted self-explanation: g = 0.55 across 69 effect sizes. Bisra et al. 2018
  • Problem solving before instruction: g = 0.36 across 53 studies. Sinha & Kapur 2021
  • Interactive > constructive > active > passive. Chi & Wylie 2014 (ICAP)

Questions are a delivery mechanism. The active ingredient is the work the student does.

Part 1 · What “Socratic” means16
[0:12–0:13] Why would questioning style not matter much? Because the questions were never the point. Chi's study is the one I keep coming back to. Tutors were told they could not explain, only prompt. Students learned just as well. And in ordinary sessions only fifteen percent of tutor comments were interactive at all. Tutors talk a lot. VanLehn's seven experiments held content constant and found dialogue beat reading only when novices were working above their level. Self-explanation, productive failure and ICAP all point the same way: the student has to build something. So when I say Socratic for the rest of this talk, read it as "a way of getting the student to do the constructing." A tutor that asks a lot of questions the student cannot use is not doing that. Calibrating expectations

A realistic target is 0.3 to 0.8 SD, not two sigma

Kraft, Schueler & Falken (2024): programmes at policy scale deliver one-third to one-half of the full-sample effect. Von Hippel (2024) on the two sigma claim.

Part 1 · What “Socratic” means17
[0:13–0:14] Before we look at any LLM paper, it helps to know what number to expect. Every second AI-tutoring pitch cites Bloom's two sigma. Bloom bundled tutoring with mastery learning, the studies were small dissertations, and it has never been replicated. Von Hippel's line is that it seems rash to promise two sigma from AI when human tutoring rarely produced it. Here is what the meta-analyses actually say. Human tutoring against no tutoring, about 0.8. Step-based tutoring systems, nearly the same. That is VanLehn's interaction plateau: finer than step-level interaction does not buy more. AutoTutor, a dialogue tutor, about 0.8 over reading the same text. Then it falls off: tutoring RCTs around 0.37, college ITS around a third, and K-12 maths ITS close to zero. So the honest band is 0.3 to 0.8, depending on the control and the test. Keep that band in mind. The same literature supplies the brakes

Eliciting helps only when the student can construct

  • Assistance dilemma: when to give help and when to withhold it. The field has no criteria. Koedinger & Aleven 2007
  • Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices can hurt experienced learners. Kalyuga et al. 2003
  • Productive failure reverses for younger learners and domain-general skills. Sinha & Kapur 2021
  • Coaching help-seeking changed how students asked, not what they learned. Aleven et al. 2016
  • "Do you understand?" almost always gets a yes, so a tutor cannot rely on it. VanLehn et al. 2005

A working definition

Elicit first.
Tell when eliciting stops paying off.

Measure it by the student's share of the constructive work, not by how many tutor turns end in a question mark.

Part 1 · What “Socratic” means18
[0:14–0:15] The same literature that says "make the student construct" also says when that stops working. Koedinger and Aleven called it the assistance dilemma in 2007: give help or withhold it, and nobody had criteria for which. Expertise reversal says guidance that helps a novice can hurt someone who already knows the material. Productive failure reverses for younger learners. And a tutor that asks "do you understand?" will almost always hear yes. So the definition I will use for the rest of the talk is on the right. Elicit first. Tell when eliciting stops paying off. Not "never tell." Hold onto that, because later in part two we will see LLM tutors fail in both directions: telling too early, and refusing to tell long after it stopped paying off. Part 2 · Built and broken21
[0:16–0:18] The four families, roughly in the order they appeared. First, keep the model and wrap a plan around it: a finite-state script, a transition graph, or an explicit decision step. Bridge is my favourite here: make the model first state the error, the strategy and the intention, then respond. Cheap and interpretable, but it gets brittle over long conversations. Second, fine-tune on Socratic dialogues that other LLMs generate. Small models learn to act Socratically, but they get measurably worse at the maths itself. Third, preference optimisation: show the model good and bad tutor moves. It reliably beats plain fine-tuning. Fourth, the 2025 and 2026 frontier: train the tutor with reinforcement learning over whole conversations against a simulated student. Best numbers so far. But notice the last column. Every one of those numbers comes from a simulated student and a model judge. We will come back to whether that matters. Spoiler: it does. The problem, named

Refusal-under-knowledge

Classical LLM safety

Refuse content the model should not produce.

The model is trained away from it. The user's request is illegitimate. Refusing is the win condition. Coldness is acceptable.

A tutor

Refuse content it should and can produce.

The model knows the answer. The student can see that it knows. The request is legitimate and sympathetic. And refusing is only half the win — the other half is that the student makes progress anyway.

Four things make it hard at once:

  • the model knows, and the student knows it knows;
  • the student is frustrated, out of time, and asking directly — a sympathetic ask;
  • a cold refusal fails: the student opens a browser tab and gets the answer unguarded, which is the exact harm;
  • so the system must withhold and stay warm and useful, every turn, under pressure.
Part 5 · Building one that withholds36
[0:36–0:37] I want to give this problem a name, because I think it is distinct and under-studied: refusal-under-knowledge. Classical LLM safety refuses content the model should not produce. There the user's request is illegitimate, refusal is the win condition, and if the refusal is cold, fine. A tutor faces the converse, and it is harder in four specific ways. The model knows the answer. The student can see that it knows — there is no plausible "I can't do that." The request is legitimate and sympathetic: it is eleven at night, the assignment is due, they are stuck. And — this is the part that makes it an engineering problem rather than a policy problem — a cold refusal actively fails, because the student has an unguarded chatbot one tab away. Over-refusing does not fail safe. It fails into the harm condition. So the requirement is: withhold, and be warm, and be genuinely useful, on every turn, including the turns where someone is actively trying to extract the answer.

Why not just write a better prompt

Two reasons a prompt cannot carry this

1. The objective is overloaded, and the parts interfere

One block of prose asked to be simultaneously warm, Socratic, never-revealing, and factually grounded in course material. Under adversarial pressure these trade against each other, and the model resolves the conflict — silently, differently every time, and usually in favour of being helpful.

2. You cannot verify a prompt by reading it

There is no property of the text "never reveal the solution" that tells you what the model does on turn six of an escalating conversation. The only way to know is to run student behaviour — including extraction attempts — through it and look at what comes out.

Both point the same way: put the binding decision outside the generating model, and calibrate it against evidence. Those are the two halves of the talk.

Caveat I will return to: this is an argument, not a measurement. The prompt-only baseline that would test it is the one experiment I have not run.

Open problems

What nobody has measured yet

  1. A Socratic vs direct-answer LLM on a delayed, unassisted test. Or over a full academic year.
  2. Whether simulator and judge rankings predict human learning. The RL frontier depends on it.
  3. When to escalate from asking to telling, and how that depends on prior knowledge.
  4. Sycophancy toward wrong student answers in a tutoring setting.
  5. Enforcement compared head to head (prompt, detector, judge, fine-tuning) on one population, with learning outcomes.
  6. Anything in upper-level computing. And Claude specifically: every peer-reviewed CS deployment I found used OpenAI models.

The one long-horizon result we have, the Chinese learning penalty emerging over about two years, is not randomised. Short trials are probably biased toward optimism.

What I would run, and where I am stuck

The study

Pre-registered, one course offering, three arms

  • no tutor
  • supervised tutor (withholding enforced)
  • explain mode — same system, instructor toggle up, ceiling at H7

The third arm is the point: same interface, same persona, same retrieval, withholding is the only thing that differs.

Primary outcome

A delayed, tool-removed assessment on goal-aligned items, ~2 weeks after the unit. Following Bastani et al. — the harm only appears once the tool is gone.

Secondary: depth to convergence, voluntary-usage persistence, and an equity slice by incoming ability — the intervention should narrow gaps, and I want to know if it widens them.

  • Judge calibration. What is the unit a human rates — the turn? the rung assignment? Who rates, and what reliability would satisfy you?

If you keep three things

Takeaways

1. Withholding protects; it rarely accelerates

Expect about 0.2 SD on an unassisted test from the better designs, not two sigma. The gains come from structure: diagnosis, curriculum, brevity, and help that arrives at the right moment. If the alternative is an unguarded chatbot, a guarded tutor is still the right call — it removes a measured harm.

2. Put the irreversible decision in code, not in a prompt

Prompt-only withholding leaks, and strict withholding over-questions. A help ceiling computed by ordinary code, outside the generating model, is injection-proof past the no-code line and unit-testable, and no later prompt edit can silently weaken it.

3. Make every rejection state its reason — and measure learning without the AI, later

Sorting failures by cause is what turns a score into a diagnosis, and it caught my own instrument being wrong. Expect failures gross-to-subtle: full solutions → almost-code → invented citations → the bug named in plain English. And every cheap measure — benchmarks, judges, simulated students, satisfaction — is a proxy none of us has shown predicts learning.

[1:05–1:06] Three things to keep. One, from the evidence: withholding protects, it rarely accelerates. Budget for about 0.2 SD from a well-designed tutor and look to structure for the gains. That is not an argument against guardrails — against an unguarded chatbot, the guardrail removes a real measured harm. It is an argument against expecting the guardrail to do the teaching. Two, from building one: put the irreversible decision in code, not in a prompt. Prompt-only withholding leaks and strict withholding over-questions, and the way out of both is a policy computed outside the prompt. If you build anything with a model in it, that is the transferable move. It is not evidence that a single student learned a single thing.

For the discussion

Three questions I would like help with

1. What outcome would convince you that withholding causes durable learning?

Immediate test, delayed unassisted test, transfer, course grades? Almost every LLM-tutor trial reports the cheapest one — and you cannot blind a student to their own tool while the control arm has an unguarded chatbot one tab away. Is a delayed, tool-removed assessment enough on its own? Is a waitlist better than an arm I believe causes harm?

2. Who should decide when to stop asking and start telling?

The student (on demand), the model, or the system (impasse length, prior knowledge)? The real-session data say the system; students say otherwise. And with an unguarded chatbot one tab away, is withholding even the right frame — or should we design for steering, which raised later scores in four of six experiments?

3. Is the over-help ladder a finding, or an artefact of my fix order?

The only test I have is to hand the apparatus to someone else, let them fix in a different order, and see whether the same rungs appear. That is expensive. Is there a cheaper one? (And the related one: how would you calibrate a model judge to a standard you would accept — what rated unit, how many raters, what agreement statistic on an ordinal scale, and does it matter that judge and judged share a model family?)
